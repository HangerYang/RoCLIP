RoCLIP

Robust contrastive language-image pretraining against data poisoning and backdoor attacks

The first method that defends CLIP against data poisoning and backdoor attacks during pre-training.

Instead of trusting each image's own caption, RoCLIP matches it to its nearest neighbor in a pool of random captions, which breaks the link an attacker planted.

Overview

CLIP learns by pulling each image toward its own caption. That is exactly what an attacker exploits: pair a handful of deer photos with captions about trucks, and CLIP learns to call deer trucks. Supervised models need around 1% of the data poisoned for this to work; CLIP needs about 0.0001%.

RoCLIP stops trusting the pairing. It keeps a large, constantly refreshed pool of captions, and every few epochs it matches each image to the caption in the pool that it is already closest to, rather than to the caption it came with. Clean images still find captions about their own content. A poisoned deer image finds deer captions, or random ones, but almost never the attacker's truck caption, so the planted association is never learned.

No clean data or clean pre-trained model is required. RoCLIP trains from scratch on the poisoned data, and because the pool keeps exposing each image to many related captions, it also learns better representations: linear-probe accuracy improves on 9 of 10 downstream datasets.

Method

RoCLIP is a drop-in change to CLIP pre-training with three parts.

LRoCLIP = InfoNCE( z̄iI, znn(i)T ),   nn(i) = argminp ∈ pool ‖ z̄iI − z̄pT ‖

The image's own caption is replaced by its nearest neighbor in the pool; negatives still come from the mini-batch.

Why poisons lose their grip

Early in training, a poisoned image still looks like what it is: a deer. Its nearest captions in the pool are about deer, or are unrelated, and almost never the attacker's truck caption. Since the image is never pulled toward that caption, the attack has nothing to learn from.

Why clean data gains

A clean image's nearest pool caption usually describes the same kind of content, often in different words. Matching against many related captions, together with image and text augmentation, gives CLIP a richer signal than its single paired caption, which is where the linear-probe gains come from.
